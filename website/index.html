Server info

Three things, done properly: talking, showing your screen, writing.

Voice

Opus at 48 kHz in 20 ms frames, tuned for speech. RNNoise noise suppression runs on your device, and echo cancellation on the desktop and on Android.

  • Push to talk, voice activation or always on, with system-wide push-to-talk and mute/deafen hotkeys
  • In-band FEC rebuilds a lost packet from the one that follows it
  • An adaptive jitter buffer grows from 40 to 160 ms only when the network needs it
  • Per-person volume, 0–400% microphone gain, a mixing desk with effects per lane
codec
Opus, 48 kHz mono, 20 ms, 48 kbps, DTX
loss repair
in-band FEC, then concealment
audio i/o
ALSA · WASAPI · Oboe (Android)

Screen sharing

H.264 encoded on your graphics card (NVENC, QuickSync or AMF), with a software fallback. Every client can watch, browsers included; H.265 is one setting away when everyone is on a desktop client.

  • 480p, 720p or 1080p at 30 or 60 fps, 1.5–7.5 Mbps, adapting to what the link carries
  • Share from a browser too: Chromium encodes H.264, Firefox VP9
  • Desktop audio comes along, as 64 kbps Opus
  • Watch it big with the chat beside it, pop it out, or pinch to zoom on a phone
  • 1280-byte packets: it fits inside WireGuard and OpenVPN tunnels
capture
PipeWire portal · Windows.Graphics.Capture · getDisplayMedia
decode
FFmpeg · AMediaCodec (Android) · WebCodecs

Chat & servers

Channel messages and direct messages ride the Signal Protocol: the relay passes on ciphertext it cannot read. History is optional and kept on your own disk, encrypted. A server with accounts can also keep end-to-end-encrypted copies for you, if you choose.

  • Several servers open at once; text channels are subscriptions, voice is one channel at a time
  • Password-protected channels, invites and pokes, encrypted too
  • Messages that delete themselves after a while, per channel or per conversation
  • Files and pictures, sealed on your device; nothing downloads until you press Load
  • Replies, threads, Markdown, reactions, and editing what you wrote
  • Persistent rooms from a channels.json on the server

Proximity & games

A channel can place voices in space: 2D on a floor plan, or 3D with height. Distance makes a voice quieter, direction moves it left or right, a wall muffles it: the same maths Mumble and TeamSpeak use, done on your own machine. Positions are encrypted with the channel key like voice, so the relay passes them on blind.

  • A virtual room: arrange everyone yourself, or share your own position with the channel; presets for a round table, a class room, a line
  • A game SDK: a mod drives positions over a local socket, with thirteen radio and phone chains and a ready-made FiveM resource with radio and calls
  • One speaker, heard several ways: a colleague two metres away who keys a radio arrives twice, over the air and as themselves
  • Yours to allow: broadcasting your position and pressing your push to talk are separate switches, both off by default

Server rules

What every VoIPC relay does, read straight off it.

RuleValueWhat it means
relay.reads_messages0Messages ride the Signal Protocol (X3DH, Double Ratchet, sender keys) through the official libsignal crate. The relay forwards ciphertext.
relay.reads_media0Voice, video and positions are AES-256-GCM per packet, with per-channel keys handed out over pairwise Signal sessions. The relay decodes nothing.
relay.storagememoryWithout accounts it keeps nothing it can read. Messages, voice and positions pass through memory; files posted in chat sit on its disk only as ciphertext, for a day at most by default, and every restart wipes them. A restart is a clean slate, but for the bans an admin set.
accountsoff · optionalTurned on by the operator, it keeps a database: names, sign-ins, who wrote where and when, and ciphertext it cannot open.
transporttls 1.3Everything rides one QUIC connection; self-signed certificates are pinned on first connect, and a changed fingerprint stops the connection.
historylocalOptional, on your own disk: PBKDF2 at 600,000 iterations and AES-256-GCM, a fresh salt and nonce per file.
telemetry0None, in the apps, the relay or on this page: no analytics, no cookies.
auditnoneVoIPC has not had an independent security audit. The code is MIT-licensed and small enough to read: audit it yourself.

Coming from 1.0? 1.3 speaks a new protocol: update your server first, then the apps. A 1.3 app cannot connect to a 1.0 server, and does not go back to 1.0 by itself. The browser needs nothing; Android and the .deb install over the old one.

Browsers need Chrome 97, Edge 98, Firefox 130 or Safari 26.4 (WebTransport and WebCodecs). Every release is signed and listed in SHA256SUMS; how to check one is in the changelog.

Host one

One static binary, a certificate, and port 9987.

# the relay, ready-built for Linux x86_64
$ curl -LO https://github.com/luki4fun/voipc/releases/download/v1.5.0/voipc-server-linux-x86_64
$ chmod +x voipc-server-linux-x86_64

# a self-signed certificate for this machine's name and address
# (browsers check them: set HOST and IP to the public ones if they differ)
$ HOST=$(hostname -f) IP=$(ip route get 1 | grep -o 'src [0-9.]*' | cut -d' ' -f2)
$ mkdir -p certs && openssl req -x509 -newkey ec \
    -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:prime256v1 \
    -keyout certs/server.key -out certs/server.crt \
    -days 365 -nodes -subj "/CN=voipc" \
    -addext "subjectAltName=DNS:$HOST,IP:$IP"

# run: UDP 9987 for every client, TCP 9987 for the browser page
$ ./voipc-server-linux-x86_64

Configure it with flags or a server.toml passed with -c; a channels.json beside it adds rooms that stay. Set admin_token, or take the one printed in the log, to moderate.

The same binary serves the browser client over HTTPS on the TCP port, so a friend with nothing installed opens the server's address on port 9987 in a browser and joins, as with the demo. Where UDP is blocked, --tcp-fallback tunnels the call over TCP.
